Suggested direction

A family secret, mapped through postcards

Your great-aunt has been tracing the family’s Nordic origins. Her first postcard arrives with a locked field bag. Every solved section of the bag reveals more dated postcards from different places.

Players use the cards as ordinary clues along the way. Near the end they find a full map and realize the entire trip has been building one final answer.

Keep the original ending

The final route code opens the travel wallet. Inside is the plane ticket from your idea deck, plus: “I could use someone who notices what everyone else walks past.”

The ticket is a fictional game prop. The exact route, final code, family connection and dig location remain open.

The reason for the locks

Your aunt loves setting family challenges. This parcel is her invitation test. The locks protect each stage of her research and release the journey a few locations at a time.

The recurring “aha”

Each postcard appears useful once, so players keep it without knowing its final purpose. The map changes the cards from souvenirs into plotted points. Connecting the dated locations reveals the final digits.

Meta structure. Start with one postcard. Release later cards across several puzzles. Give the full map near the end. The final clue should imply that every stop mattered without explicitly saying “sort by date and draw lines.” A hint ladder can reveal those actions only if needed.

Why keep this simple?

It teaches players to trust the supplied reference cards and gives access to the richer physical tasks. In the real bag, the key can be split across a catalogue card and the object tag.

This is her invented digit cipher using rune shapes. It is not a historical rune-to-number system.

The king’s escape

Move the king to a gold corner in exactly three moves. Slide any distance horizontally or vertically. Dark pieces stay fixed. No jumping or captures. Read the digit where each move ends.

A custom solo puzzle inspired by hnefatafl. These simplified rules are printed on the prop. No knowledge of the full game required.

Decisions for the next draft

Make the bag feel like it belongs to her

Core physical kit

  • A field bag with clearly marked locked pouches.
  • A set of dated postcards, with only the first card outside the bag.
  • A full journey map released near the end. The route must be tested before locations are fixed.
  • Replica object labels, family record cards and a reusable notebook.
  • A folding 7 × 7 board with one movable king. Print the fixed pieces for easy reset.
  • Saga strips, a travel wallet and a fictional boarding pass.

Postcard count, full route, final code, bag, lock inventory, materials and dimensions are unconfirmed.

An optional website with a clear job

“Aunt’s Field Archive” holds her welcome message, three levels of hints per puzzle, and a closing message after the wallet opens.

Let physical locks control progress. The website adds atmosphere and help without becoming a second puzzle system. A printed hint booklet covers the same information.

Later option: the boarding pass opens an illustrated dig briefing. Audio always has a transcript. This page is the design guide, not that player website.

Other directions worth keeping

More magic: the sleeping raven

A final message hints that the dig has uncovered something alive.

Keep the ticket reveal and make the supernatural layer a final tease.

More tactile: thread on the map

Players pin each postcard location and connect the stops with cord instead of drawing directly on the map.

This makes the last clue physical and resettable. Prototype pin spacing and route legibility before choosing the locations.

More investigation: the wrong museum label

Compare a drawing, a repair note and a catalogue entry to identify a mislabeled replica. Its accession number opens the archive.

A swap for family records if you want less genealogy.

Three useful choices

  1. Tone: grounded mystery, subtle magic, or full mythic quest?
  2. Audience: children with adults, teens, or adults?
  3. Final map: draw with pencil, connect pins with cord, or use a transparent overlay?

Working assumptions for this draft: a small mixed-age group, no prior Norse knowledge, a table available, and reusable props. No duration or difficulty claim yet.

The route is now the hero prop. Engineer the path first, then assign plausible dated stops to its vertices. That prevents the geography from producing unreadable digits.

Reset and prototype checks

Repack the wallet first, then the WELL pouch and two branch pouches. Put the remaining materials in the archive and main compartment. Leave only postcard 01 outside. Scramble every lock. Keep a packing photo and a numbered spare postcard set.

Before final production: verify the three opening digits at print size; make every date and place name unambiguous; confirm every route point is easy to locate; test that the completed lines resemble only the intended digits; run an unfamiliar group through the paper version. Observe solve times before choosing a duration target.
