Working direction

A family secret, carried by postcards

Your great-aunt Liv has been tracing the family’s Nordic origins. Her first postcard arrives with her locked field bag. Solving what she left behind releases more postcards from more places.

The postcards are the game. Everything else in the bag exists to act on them: the comb reads a message, the balance orders three cards, the board is set up by one. Players use the cards as ordinary clues along the way, and at the end the whole deck becomes one final answer.

Keep the original ending

The final route code opens the last container. Inside is the plane ticket from your idea deck, plus: “I could use someone who notices what everyone else walks past.”

The ticket is a fictional game prop. The candidate routes form 1972: Leif 1, Rollo’s family line 9, Aud 7 and Harald 2. Her travel year is now dated and verified — 2 Feb to 15 Dec, variable stay lengths, in the Final riddle tab. The printed maps are now settled (PZ-06): four laminated letter sheets, drawn on with wet-erase marker. The dig is in Norway, but the site itself and what it is for are still open (ST-05). Decoy postcards and the family crest/logo filter are now part of the candidate ending too — see the Final riddle tab.

Why it is locked at all

Liv loves setting family challenges. This parcel is her invitation test. Each stage protects a piece of her research and releases the journey a few places at a time.

The recurring “aha”

Every card does a job once, so players keep it. At the end every card does a second job, and the deck they have been carrying turns out to be the map of her whole trip.

This sequence is deliberately incomplete. Two anchors are settled — the first puzzle and the last. Everything between them is open, and so is the number and naming of the containers the bag is divided into. That is on purpose: the middle should be designed out of the postcard system rather than fitted around a pocket list decided in advance. Candidates live in the Options tab; everything unresolved is tracked in Open questions.

What we know

Two anchors

First puzzle · a four-digit code

The bag arrives locked, with the first postcard and two luggage tags attached to it. Each tag has a printed “If found, please return to” line that Liv never filled in properly — instead she hand-wrote the address of a hotel she stayed at, and the street number is the only number on the tag.

Postcard L1 is from L’Anse aux Meadows and names the place. The tag addressed to a L’Anse aux Meadows hotel therefore carries the first two digits; the other tag carries the last two.

Code 1021 — the year tree-ring dating placed the Norse at L’Anse aux Meadows. Tag A: Vinland Trail Lodge, 10 Skipper’s Wharf, L’Anse aux Meadows, NL. Tag B: Hôtel Rollon, 21 avenue Rollo, Rouen, France. Note what the postcard is doing: it supplies the ordering rule, not the data. That is the standard the rest of the game should hold to.

Last puzzle · four maps and 22 postcards

Players finish with four printed maps and 22 postcards — the 18 real stops plus one decoy per trail. A note in the final bundle (the family logo and crest) lets players filter out the four decoys before tracing. Placing each trail’s real cards and drawing its legs in the right order traces a shape, and the four shapes are the four digits of the final code.

Candidate code 1972: Leif 1, Rollo 9, Aud 7, Harald 2. The shapes still have to be tested at true print size — the family 9 is the riskiest. Whether the cards are pinned, connected with cord, or read under an overlay is undecided. The 22-card, decoy-filtered version is a candidate direction, not yet proofed — see the Final riddle tab.

1 · The deck and the filter

22 cards, four of them decoys

One decoy per trail

Each trail gets one decoy postcard: a place Liv really visited that is not part of that storyline. Decoys do earlier puzzle jobs like every other card, so nothing marks them as unused ahead of time. Decided, 15 Sept 2026: that job has to be a real lock, the same standard every other card meets, not just flavour or a calendar statement — see the Postcard system tab. Brattahlíð already meets it (the beasts-chain imprint); Walcheren now does too, combined with Châlus and Roumare in the "counting in the illustration" lock (PZ-19); Bjarnarhöfn now does too, paired with the comb to gate entry to Harald's leg (PZ-17); Constantinople is now assigned too — it carries one third of the MEAD riddle (PZ-20), a rebus for FERMENTED, alongside H2 and H3.

Which drawing covers which trail

The 3-element symbol covers the two 3-stop trails (Leif, Aud); the 6-element crest covers the two 6-stop trails (Rollo, Harald). Each real trail contains every element of its drawing exactly once, so players can confirm the filter before ordering anything.

Decided, 15 Sept 2026 — elements: symbol = raven, longship, compass rose/sun-wheel. Crest = battle-axe, shield, wolf, anchor, drinking horn, valknut. Which specific element sits on which real stop’s card is still open.

Fake elements can’t be counted out

The Leif and Aud decoys share one fake element; the Rollo and Harald decoys share another. Every element in the deck appears exactly twice, and only the drawings expose which two are fake. Fakes must look like they belong on a crest, so they can’t be dismissed on style alone.

Decided, 15 Sept 2026 — fakes: Leif/Aud decoys share Mjölnir (Thor’s hammer). Rollo/Harald decoys share a crown — chosen because both trails are about rulers, which is exactly what makes it convincing.

The element mark itself

Visually separate from the stamp (trail) and the postmark (place) — for example a wax-seal impression or a small mark in the picture border. Deliberately not UV: the blacklight prop arrives early, and players would scan every card before the rule means anything. Needs a legibility test at card size under table lighting.

Decoys still have to earn their place on the map

Each decoy city must sit on its trail’s map so that including it draws a plausible wrong digit — that’s what stops a team from eyeballing the 1 and the 7 straight off the map.

Decided, 15 Sept 2026 — decoy cities: Leif = Brattahlíð / Qassiarsuk, Greenland. Rollo = Walcheren, Netherlands. Aud = Bjarnarhöfn, Iceland. Harald = Constantinople/Istanbul. All four are real Liv-visited places already in the research corpus (stops.js), just not on that trail’s six postcard stops. None is shape-checked against its map projection yet — that test, and the four decoy cards’ text, are still open.

2 · The itinerary

Liv’s travel year

2 Feb – 15 Dec · variable stays

Decided 17 Sept 2026, replacing the 3-day slot grid and its 1 March–3 May window. Four trails interleaved across one ten-month research year: 94 nights away out of 316 days, so she travels in bursts and goes home between legs — which is also why the cards arrive in batches, and why card L1 can say she has spent two years on this.

Why the slot grid had to go, and what replaced it. Under a uniform 3-day slot a date was a position — (date − start) ÷ 3 + 1 — so a single dated ticket excluded every other card from that slot, and that arithmetic carried most of the deduction. Variable stays destroy it: a date now tells you only the order of the dated props among themselves. The previous clue set was re-run under the weaker semantics and collapsed to 18+ answers across more than 400,000 valid calendars. The set below is rebuilt to be purely ordinal — the order is carried by what Liv writes, not by arithmetic on dates. That is precisely what frees the stay lengths to vary. Two rules are retired with the grid: “every stated gap is a multiple of 3 days” (no clue states a gap in days any more) and the slot-exclusion deduction.

3 · Clue types and their jobs

Each type does one job; none carries the puzzle alone

Museum tickets · anchors

One per trail, reusing tickets players already hold: L’Anse 2 Feb, Rouen 17 Mar, Aud’s Treasure Museum 13 Apr (PZ-17), Hnefatafl/Oslo 28 Apr. Their job changed on 17 Sept 2026. Under the old 3-day grid a ticket date fixed a card to a slot and excluded every other card from it — the Einstein-style deduction. With variable stay lengths a date is no longer a position, so the four tickets now establish only the order of the dated props among themselves. That is still one real job: Aud’s ticket predating Oslo’s is what proves Aud first-appears before Harald, and therefore that the third digit is Aud’s 7 and not Harald’s 2. Aud’s ticket doesn’t carry a date yet; it currently only feeds the treasure-map code, not this calendar mechanism. Since 17 Sept 2026 it is also the route-record card — its reverse is the seven-row form players write each stop’s plot number into, with row 1 pre-filled (PZ-17).

Postcard statements · relative order

These now carry the whole deduction — see the clue set above. In Liv’s voice, not as instructions: a mix of immediate follow-ons, loose “some time after” comparisons, and cross-trail references (“between Rouen and Bayeux I slipped north to Iceland”). Loose wording must clearly mean “later” — players read “after” as “right after.”

Final bundle · resolvers

Two or three materially different items. Cross-trail links are the strongest — a flight such as Oslo → Reykjavík fixes a Harald stop in slot n and an Aud stop in slot n+1, and can sit anywhere in either trail, not only at the ends. A taxi or train receipt naming landmarks rather than cities forces a map lookup. The crest/logo note is also in this bundle.

Gating target

Before the bundle arrives, players can place much of the calendar but not settle the Rollo and Harald orders. Without the bundle, several answers survive; with it, exactly one.

Design risk. By script, not by eye

Run 17 Sept 2026

Exactly one answer survives, where “answer” means the order within each trail plus first-appearance order. Proven analytically — each trail’s order is forced by its clues — and confirmed over the first 400,000 valid calendars. The full calendar is not unique and does not need to be: cross-trail interleaving is almost entirely free, so many calendars give the same answer.

2 · Bundle actually gates pass

Without the bundle, 3 answers survive: H5 Aci Castello floats inside Harald’s leg. The bundle’s Hamburg → Catania boarding pass is the single ordering resolver. The crest/logo note in the same bundle is the absolute gate — the solver assumes filtering has already happened, so without that note the endgame cannot begin at all.

3 · No decoy reads as a shape not run

This one is geometric, not logical — it needs each decoy city plotted on its trail’s real map projection to see what digit the wrong route draws. The solver cannot answer it. Still open under PZ-18, together with the tension that Guessing risks above wants a decoy to draw a plausible wrong digit while this test wants it to draw none.

4 · Rules hold pass

First appearances run Leif, Rollo, Aud, Harald, at positions 1, 4, 6 and 8. The old “every stated gap is a multiple of 3 days” rule is retired with the slot grid — no clue in the current set states a gap in days at all, so the rule has nothing to constrain.

Two findings worth keeping from the solver runs. First, individually redundant clues are not jointly redundant: six clues each tested as droppable on their own, and removing all six together took the answer count from 1 to 8. Never prune the clue table on drop-one results alone. Second, two clue-set shapes were tried and rejected on evidence — moving Rollo’s closing clue into the bundle as a dated receipt (17 answers; a date pins R6’s position but not its rank, so R3–R5 can still follow it), and resolving Harald’s tail from the bundle alone with no added card clause (6 answers).
This supersedes the postmark-date endgame and the plan to hold back Rollo, Aud and Harald cards (PZ-11, now superseded — decoys provide the same early-drawing protection, provided each decoy draws a plausible wrong digit). The brief

A card does three jobs

1 · It is a map point

The stamp motif gives the trail; a small element mark gives whether the card counts toward that trail's shape; the postmark gives the place only. All 22 feed the final reveal: split by stamp, filter out the four decoys with the crest/logo note, reconstruct the order from ticket/postcard/final-bundle evidence, draw the legs, read the four digits. Candidate — see Final riddle tab

2 · It is worth reading

Real trivia about a real place, in a titled Fun Fact block (was the printed caption — renamed and moved when the address block shrank to make room for it). Written on card L1 (the butternut wood found far from any butternut tree) — the template for the other seventeen.

3 · It carries a clue

Alone or in a group. Her message sets a task, or four cards have to be physically lined up before they say anything at all.

Every card is active. All 22 do a job besides the final ordering, including the four decoys. This replaces the earlier “five or six active cards, the rest quiet” decision, which is why the sparse-channel worksheet has been removed from the Design spec tab.
Decided, 15 Sept 2026 — every card's job 3 gates a lock. Sharpens the rule above: a card's clue can't just be flavour, a calendar statement, or an element mark on its own — it has to feed an actual physical lock, the same standard cards L1–L3 and Rouen/Bayeux/Winchester/Battle already meet. This applies to decoys too: Brattahlíð already does (it carries the real beasts-chain imprint), Walcheren now does too, alongside Châlus and Roumare — all three combine into the "counting in the illustration" lock (PZ-19) — and Bjarnarhöfn now does too, paired with the comb to gate entry to Harald's leg (PZ-17). Currently short of this standard: Constantinople, the one still-unassigned decoy.
The craft idea

Every card carries two voices

This is how real picture postcards work, and it is what lets jobs 2 and 3 sit on one small piece of card without either getting in the other’s way.

Fun Fact, typed · the publisher’s
L’ANSE AUX MEADOWS, NEWFOUNDLAND — The only confirmed Norse settlement in North America, excavated 1961–1968 by Helge and Anne Stine Ingstad. Eight timber-and-sod structures. Édition Vinland Nº 14.
Job: facts, and the numbers that hide in them. Dry, third-person, small type in the titled Fun Fact block, on all 18 — nobody suspects a caption.
Handwritten · hers
Third time here and the wind still finds the one gap in my coat. Don’t believe the eight structures — I counted seven and an argument. Ask your father about the comb.
Job: voice, tasks, and the rules. Which cards, in what order, what to do. The quirk lives entirely here.

The split does quiet work. A typed Fun Fact can carry a number without ever looking planted, because it is a genuine fact about a genuine place. And because she argues with it in her own handwriting elsewhere on the card, she can point at exactly the number she wants read without ever writing “use this number.”

Decided. Fun Fact is always typed, on every card — never handwritten. Keeps the two voices doing one job each: typed for facts and hidden numbers, her handwriting for voice, tasks and rules. PC-12 closed.

The toolbox

What a deck of cards can do that a sheet of clues cannot

If the postcards are the game, these are the game’s verbs. Each is physical, each is resettable, and each costs nothing beyond print and a pair of scissors.

A
Line four up and read across the seams
Her sentence runs off the right edge of one card and continues on the next. Self-verifying — the sentence either reads or it does not, so no lock is needed to confirm it. Worked out below.
B
Hold two together against a lamp
Print one card’s back on lighter stock; overlaid on another and lit from behind, marks from both combine into a third thing. The same instinct as a lithophane, at no cost.
C
Lay the comb across a message
The grille already in the design. Broken teeth expose certain words; rotated or flipped, one comb reads several cards. Reuse is the point — one prop across two stages is harder for players and cheaper to build.
D
Argue with the Fun Fact
Her handwriting contradicts the typed facts. Points at a number without saying “use this number,” and it is genuinely funny.

Format: purchased, never sent

Each card is addressed and stamped like a real postcard rather than a plain note, which is what makes an unmailed stamp collection plausible in the first place: travellers buy postcards and never send half of them. It also earns back an address block as usable clue space, which a note-card format would give up for nothing.

The recipient is fictional: John Ericson, 24 Longship Way, Ottawa ON K1L 1S1, Canada. "Longship Way" doesn't exist as an Ottawa street, so the address can't collide with a real occupied building regardless of the postal code. Ericson doubles as a quiet nod to Leif without being tied to his trail specifically — it reads as the family surname.

The same stamp series is reused across countries within a trail; explained, if it ever needs explaining, as the aunt buying a batch of stamps at the first post office of each leg. This is ordinary traveller behaviour and doesn't need to be stated in-fiction.

Other postcard mechanics worth using

  • Grille overlay. A card with windows cut in it laid over a message, exposing only certain words. One grille rotated or flipped decodes several cards. See the comb prop below.
  • Hole-punch stack. Punched holes plus a registration notch; stacked in the right order against a window, only certain letters on the bottom card stay visible. The order is the puzzle, and it rehearses the skill the final map needs.
  • Edge continuation. Horizon lines that continue between cards, with something written across the seams and legible only when the joint is closed.
  • Fold lines. A crease brings two halves of a message together to form a sentence present in neither half.
  • Counting in the illustration. Boats, birds, windows. Trivial on its own — only ship it with a non-obvious ordering rule attached.

Puzzles & locks

One place for every puzzle that has been worked out: the lock it feeds, the code it produces, and links straight to the props and postcards that carry its clues. Status pills say how settled each one is — decided, partly decided, open. Every entry carries the open-question IDs it is tracked under; the full reasoning and anything still unresolved lives in Open questions, which stays the single backlog.

The standard every puzzle here is held to

Moved here from the Design spec, 17 Sept 2026 — it is a rule for writing puzzles, so it belongs with the puzzles.

The Hiking backpack is the calibration. Its codes sit at the intersection of two or three props, its items arrive before their purpose is known, and the part it hides is the ordering rule rather than the data. Norse should be a notch harder than that.

The three questions

Run every puzzle through these:

  • Where do I look?
  • What do I take from it?
  • In what order?

Answering all three makes the puzzle obvious. Answering none makes it unfair. Answer two, hide one. The hidden one should almost always be the third.

Hide the rule, not the data

Numbers and letters stay in plain sight. What players have to earn is which ones to use and in what sequence. This is what the Hiking sticker puzzle does: the digits are visible, the historical-era ordering is the puzzle.

Re-read, don't re-find

The cheapest strong aha is re-reading a prop already read for a different reason. Plant ordering rules as throwaway habits in the aunt's letters, then make one of them load-bearing three puzzles later.

Two or three props per code

At least two codes in the game should need three separate props: a data source, a selection rule and an ordering rule. This is the main difficulty lever and it is cheaper than adding puzzles.

Answers self-verify

Where possible the final step should produce a word or a shape that is visibly right before the lock is touched. Hiking does this with MOON.

Soft red herrings only

Nothing is a dead end. A prop that looks irrelevant now must be load-bearing later. Never spend a player's attention without eventually repaying it.

Supply every table

No outside knowledge, ever. Rune tables, coin references and board rules all ship inside the bag, the way Hiking ships a periodic table and a calculator.

Hint ladder. Hiking runs two hints per stage. Three-prop codes need three: the first purely locational ("you already have everything — one piece is a card you have already read"), the second naming the props, the third naming the rule.

PZ-06 Decided
The final map mechanic: laminated letter prints, drawn on with wet-erase marker.
Four letter sheets, printed and laminated at home. Players sort the deck by stamp, lay each trail’s cards out beside its map in date order, and draw the legs straight onto the laminate. No pins, no cord, no overlay. Wipe to reset.
Pinned cards is eliminated on geometry, not preference. Rouen and Roumare are 10 km apart on a trail spanning 624 km, so they land 0.13 in apart on a letter sheet — and still only 0.38 in apart on a 24 × 36 poster. Two 5-inch postcards cannot occupy that gap at any printable size. Separately, six cards need 105 in² against a letter sheet’s 84 in² of printable area.
Footprint is fine: four letter sheets laid 2 × 2 come to about 17 × 22 in, roughly 1.4 × 1.8 ft, leaving a normal table clear for the cards and props. Larger shop-printed and laminated maps (tabloid at about 1.8 × 2.8 ft for the set) stay available if the drawing ever feels cramped, but they end free same-day reprints.
Specs: gloss pouches, since matte takes marker unevenly, and wet-erase rather than dry-erase — dry-erase smears under a palm resting on a sheet being drawn across, and ghosts if left for days. A laminating pouch also covers both faces at once, which is what PZ-07 needs for the map whose back carries the board setup. 5 mil pouches over 3 mil for a sheet that is drawn on and wiped repeatedly.
Per-trail colour, on the grid band only. Each sheet's border band carries its trail's colour — Leif #216580, Rollo #A2562D, Aud #6D528B, and #467444 for Harald once that sheet exists — with the grid references reversed to white. Four laminated sheets are hard to tell apart on a crowded table, and the band is the only element visible from any edge that carries no map content. Built into build_trail_maps_pdf.py as a band key per trail.
Print on white or natural cardstock, not coloured. The sheet is a full-coverage two-tone illustration (sea #DCE7E4, land #EFE3C4) and printers cannot lay down white, so coloured stock would shift both fills toward the paper and compress a contrast that is already narrow. That contrast is load-bearing rather than decorative: it is what identifies the unlabelled animals on Leif's chart, so it cannot be spent on route identity. Coloured stock is better spent on the paper props — her letters, the museum tickets and their indexes, the luggage tags — where ink coverage is light and the paper colour becomes the design instead of fighting it.
Still untested: that the drawn shapes read as 1972 in a real play-test. Harald’s 2 is the loosest of the four — now that his real sheet is built (PZ-09), the traced route reads more as a zigzag than a digit at all. Accepted anyway: by then one lock remains and the code parses as a year, which self-checks.

Route mechanic redesigned, 17 Sept 2026 — supersedes digits-baked-into-the-artwork. Everything below this note describing hidden digits worked into the landmark illustrations (4 paddles, 8 carvings, 1 gate gap, 6 broken stones) and the resulting four-digit code 4816 is superseded. It is kept in place as the record of why the change was made.

What was wrong with it. Because the digit lived in the drawing, the same drawing always yielded the same digit. Placing a chapel twice meant both chapels carried 6, so picking the wrong one cost nothing and the decoy was decorative. Making decoys bite would have meant a separate render per digit variant — roughly eighteen icons instead of nine — and it forced every clue to describe a landmark’s appearance rather than its position.

The replacement. Every cell of the detail panel carries its own small two-digit survey plot number printed quietly in one corner. Repeats across the grid are fine and expected — the numbers are a lookup table, not a signal. Players follow a clue chain from landmark to landmark, write down the plot number of each stop, and the lock code is the sum of the stops’ plot numbers. This decouples the artwork from the puzzle entirely: icons can now repeat freely, and Codex draws a landscape rather than digit-bearing icons.

Lock is now three digits, not four. Seven stops with two-digit plot numbers sum to the mid-hundreds. A four-digit result is arithmetically unreachable this way (it would need a route of well over a hundred cells), so the treasure lock drops to three dials. The code is derived from whatever the final numbering is rather than chosen up front — see the working value below.

Two rules this mechanic forces, both learned the hard way.
  • Every stop must be a different landmark type. Addition is order-independent, so two stops sharing a type can be swapped for free and still total correctly. A first pass with sheepfolds at both stop 4 and stop 6 produced three wrong routes summing to exactly the right answer. Seven stops therefore use seven distinct types.
  • The numbering must be verified, not assumed. Every near-miss route — each stop substituted for each same-type decoy on the map — is enumerated and checked. Nothing wrong may land within 15 of the answer. The same first pass had a wrong route two away from the target, indistinguishable from an arithmetic slip.

The upside of order-independence: players cannot fail by walking the route in the wrong order. Only misidentifying a landmark hurts. One whole failure mode is gone.

Card mechanics

Things a deck can do

The full menu. The ones in use are marked; the rest are available and cost nothing but print.

In use
Edge continuation
A sentence or a line runs off one card and onto the next. Self-verifying. Family A.
In use
Grille overlay
The comb laid across a message exposes certain words. Rotated or flipped, one comb decodes several cards. Family C.
In use
Hold to the light
Two cards overlaid and backlit; marks from both combine. Family B.
In use
The message sets a task
The card is the rule card for a physical prop. Family E.
Available
Hole-punch stack
Punched holes plus a registration notch; stacked in the right order against a window, only certain letters stay visible. The order is the puzzle, and it rehearses the skill the final maps need.
Available
Fold lines
A crease brings two halves of a message together to form a sentence present in neither half. Needs a card you are willing to crease — print a spare.
Available
Counting in the illustration
Boats, birds, windows. Trivial on its own — only ship it with a non-obvious ordering rule attached.
Available
Argue with the caption
Her handwriting contradicts the printed facts, pointing at a number without naming it. Free wherever a card already has both voices.
Available
Currency grouping
The denomination is in local currency, so it groups the cards by country as well as by trail. Gives a clean four (France), a pair (England) and six singletons — which are exactly Harald’s six, since his is the only trail that never repeats a country.
Dropped
Anomaly stamps
A card wearing the wrong trail’s motif. Its job was to select which cards matter; with every card active there is nothing to select. Two ideas if it is ever revived: the anomaly cards are the ones whose dates are wrong, or they are her own bookmarks for the three stops where she found something.
Dropped
Tilted stamps
A four-state channel that is fiddly to print consistently and hard to read reliably. If it ever comes back, the better job is bearings: lay each card on its map location and the stamp angle points somewhere, with four lines crossing at one point.

Cipher backbone

Branch runes (kvistrúnir)

A real Norse cipher form: a vertical stem with branches on either side. The left branches count which group a rune belongs to; the right branches count its position within that group. Every character is a pair of small numbers drawn as tally strokes.

Why this one

  • It reads as authentic carving. Nobody guesses it is a cipher before finding the key.
  • It is two-axis, so much harder than substitution — but mechanical once understood, so nobody stalls forever.
  • It suits carving straight into a map, so it hides among ordinary reference marks instead of needing a prop of its own.
  • The key splits naturally across two postcards, so the map alone is not enough.

Prop

Hnefatafl: escape path and museum ticket

Three connected pieces — a setup split across two sheets of paper, a three-move escape, and a code read from the king's own travel. Arrives with Harald's trail, via the Oslo postcard (stop 1).

The rules, and where they come from

The rules are printed matter, not her handwriting: an admission ticket from the Hnefatafl Museum in Oslo, tucked into the bag with the Oslo postcard. That keeps the voice rule intact — printed pages carry real history, her hand carries the task. The ticket explains the real historical game only — it does not describe this puzzle's own house rules, and gives no hint that this board's dark pieces never move.

Light pieces defend the king at the centre. Dark pieces start around the edges and try to trap him. Every piece slides any distance in a straight line, like a rook in chess. Trap an enemy piece between two of your own to capture it. The king escapes by reaching a corner. The attackers win by trapping him first.

The ticket can say honestly that no complete Viking-Age tafl ruleset survives and that these rules are one reconstruction. Its text is subject to HI-05 before it is printed. The museum is invented deliberately; the real Oslo institution is the Viking Ship Museum, and the prop should not borrow it.

Her task, on the Oslo postcard, in her own words: “We like to play our own rules though: can you get the king to escape to a corner in exactly three moves?” The word exactly is what forces the solution to be unique.

Built: Props/Hnefatafl/build_ticket_pdf.py → output/pdf/Hnefatafl_Ticket_Print.pdf.

The setup: one board across two papers

Liv wrote the piece positions on the back of Harald's Oslo trail map. The glue was still wet when the ticket was set down on it, and the right-hand strip lifted off — what is left on the map is an illegible stain, and the clean copy is on the ticket's blank back panel, mirrored.

  • The gap announces itself. The grid lines and column letters run into the stain, so the board is visibly seven wide and visibly missing two columns. Nobody has to deduce that a second sheet exists; the stain is the shape of the object that took the ink.
  • Reading it: hold the ticket to the light and read the offset through the paper — mirrored twice, so it reads correctly — then butt it against the map. The offset must therefore land on a panel that is blank on both sides, on stock thin enough to show through (PR-09).
  • Registration is self-verifying. The grid lines cross the join, so only one alignment makes them continue. A one-square misalignment is visible rather than silent.
  • Guessing does not produce a wrong answer. Verified by search: the full board has exactly one three-move escape, but if a player assumes the hidden strip is empty they find 18 candidate routes. The puzzle refuses to resolve instead of resolving wrongly — the failure mode you want in a bag nobody can debug mid-play.

Both the stain and the offset are printed, not real ink, so the prop is stable and reprintable. This also gives Harald's map an early-ish job once it exists: players handle it mid-game and get used to it, without learning what it is finally for.

Tools

The three decoding tools and what each is for

Each tool opens a class of clue. Each should have one primary job so it never feels decorative.

UV pen and blacklight

Primary job: the trail reading order. The order in which the four trails are read is currently "left to right," which is arbitrary — the strongest moment in the game resting on the weakest rule. Instead, write four small numerals beside the trails on the map in UV ink. The order becomes something she left for you, and the code 1972 survives untouched.

This is the highest-value use of the pen. Spend it here before anywhere else.

Red acetate filter

Primary job: the illustrations. A red-noise wash over each postcard image; under the filter, one object per card is circled or a latitude appears in the sky. Adds a clue layer with no extra prop.

Needs work Home printers vary a great deal on this. Test colour accuracy before committing any puzzle to it.

Magnifier

Primary job: the branch runes. Carve them small enough that the tally strokes are ambiguous to the naked eye. The magnifier turns "I think that's three branches" into certainty, which makes it a real tool rather than a gimmick.

Mechanic

Three non-lock redirect clues

Puzzles whose answer is an instruction rather than a code. They work best exactly where a lock would be clumsy. Three is the cap — more and the locks stop feeling like the spine.

Rules that keep them from stalling a group

  • Name a prop that is unique. There is one comb, one balance, one board. Never "the card."
  • Name a physical action, not a concept. Weigh, hold to the light, fit into, rotate, stack. Never "consider" or "compare."
  • The payoff must be visible within seconds, or players will not believe they did it right.
