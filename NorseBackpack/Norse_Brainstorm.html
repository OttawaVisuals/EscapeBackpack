Working direction

A family secret, carried by postcards

Your great-aunt Liv has been tracing the family’s Nordic origins. Her first postcard arrives with her locked field bag. Solving what she left behind releases more postcards from more places.

The postcards are the game. Everything else in the bag exists to act on them: the comb reads a message, the balance orders three cards, the board is set up by one. Players use the cards as ordinary clues along the way, and at the end the whole deck becomes one final answer.

Keep the original ending

The final route code opens the last container. Inside is the plane ticket from your idea deck, plus: “I could use someone who notices what everyone else walks past.”

The ticket is a fictional game prop. The candidate routes form 1972: Leif 1, Rollo’s family line 9, Aud 7 and Harald 2. Dates, the printed maps and the dig location remain open.

Why it is locked at all

Liv loves setting family challenges. This parcel is her invitation test. Each stage protects a piece of her research and releases the journey a few places at a time.

The recurring “aha”

Every card does a job once, so players keep it. At the end every card does a second job, and the deck they have been carrying turns out to be the map of her whole trip.

This sequence is deliberately incomplete. Two anchors are settled — the first puzzle and the last. Everything between them is open, and so is the number and naming of the containers the bag is divided into. That is on purpose: the middle should be designed out of the postcard system rather than fitted around a pocket list decided in advance. Candidates live in the Options tab; everything unresolved is tracked in Open questions.
Settled

What we know

Two anchors

First puzzle · a four-digit code

The bag arrives locked, with the first postcard and two luggage tags attached to it. Each tag has a printed “If found, please return to” line that Liv never filled in properly — instead she hand-wrote the address of a hotel she stayed at, and the street number is the only number on the tag.

Postcard 01 is from L’Anse aux Meadows and names the place. The tag addressed to a L’Anse aux Meadows hotel therefore carries the first two digits; the other tag carries the last two.

Code 1021 — the year tree-ring dating placed the Norse at L’Anse aux Meadows. Tag A: Vinland Trail Lodge, 10 Skipper’s Wharf, L’Anse aux Meadows, NL. Tag B: Hôtel Rollon, 21 avenue Rollo, Rouen, France. Note what the postcard is doing: it supplies the ordering rule, not the data. That is the standard the rest of the game should hold to.

Last puzzle · four maps and every postcard

Players finish with four printed maps and all 18 postcards. Each map carries one trail. Placing that trail’s cards and drawing its legs in the right order traces a shape, and the four shapes are the four digits of the final code.

Candidate code 1972: Leif 1, Rollo 9, Aud 7, Harald 2. The shapes still have to be tested at true print size — the family 9 is the riskiest. Whether the cards are pinned, connected with cord, or read under an overlay is undecided.

Core mechanic · work in progress

The postcard system

Eighteen postcards as the game itself, with every other prop reduced to an instrument that acts on them. This tab is the current thinking, not a finished spec — the parts that are not settled are marked, and the full list lives in Open questions.

Status. The shape below is agreed; the contents are not. The three jobs, the two voices and the verb list are the working model. The card-by-card assignment is a first pass with one acknowledged gap, and the eighteen dates, captions and digits are all unwritten.
The brief

A card does three jobs

1 · It is a map point

The stamp motif gives the trail; the postmark gives the date and the place. All eighteen feed the final reveal: split by stamp, order by date, draw the legs, read the four digits.

2 · It is worth reading

Real trivia about a real place, in a titled Fun Fact block (was the printed caption — renamed and moved when the address block shrank to make room for it). Written on card 01 (the butternut wood found far from any butternut tree) — the template for the other seventeen.

3 · It carries a clue

Alone or in a group. Her message sets a task, or four cards have to be physically lined up before they say anything at all.

Every card is active. All eighteen do a job besides the final ordering. This replaces the earlier “five or six active cards, the rest quiet” decision, which is why the sparse-channel worksheet has been removed from the Design spec tab.
The craft idea

Every card carries two voices

This is how real picture postcards work, and it is what lets jobs 2 and 3 sit on one small piece of card without either getting in the other’s way.

Fun Fact, typed · the publisher’s
L’ANSE AUX MEADOWS, NEWFOUNDLAND — The only confirmed Norse settlement in North America, excavated 1961–1968 by Helge and Anne Stine Ingstad. Eight timber-and-sod structures. Édition Vinland Nº 14.
Job: facts, and the numbers that hide in them. Dry, third-person, small type in the titled Fun Fact block, on all 18 — nobody suspects a caption.
Handwritten · hers
Third time here and the wind still finds the one gap in my coat. Don’t believe the eight structures — I counted seven and an argument. Ask your father about the comb.
Job: voice, tasks, and the rules. Which cards, in what order, what to do. The quirk lives entirely here.

The split does quiet work. A typed Fun Fact can carry a number without ever looking planted, because it is a genuine fact about a genuine place. And because she argues with it in her own handwriting elsewhere on the card, she can point at exactly the number she wants read without ever writing “use this number.”

Decided. Fun Fact is always typed, on every card — never handwritten. Keeps the two voices doing one job each: typed for facts and hidden numbers, her handwriting for voice, tasks and rules. PC-12 closed.

The toolbox

What a deck of cards can do that a sheet of clues cannot

If the postcards are the game, these are the game’s verbs. Each is physical, each is resettable, and each costs nothing beyond print and a pair of scissors.

A
Line four up and read across the seams
Her sentence runs off the right edge of one card and continues on the next. Self-verifying — the sentence either reads or it does not, so no lock is needed to confirm it. Worked out below.
B
Hold two together against a lamp
Print one card’s back on lighter stock; overlaid on another and lit from behind, marks from both combine into a third thing. The same instinct as a lithophane, at no cost.
C
Lay the comb across a message
The grille already in the design. Broken teeth expose certain words; rotated or flipped, one comb reads several cards. Reuse is the point — one prop across two stages is harder for players and cheaper to build.
D
Argue with the Fun Fact
Her handwriting contradicts the typed facts. Points at a number without saying “use this number,” and it is genuinely funny.
E
Set a task in the message
The card is the rule card. “The game we used to play — I have set it up as I left it. King to a corner in three. You never could see the second move.”
F
Fold one card
A crease brings two halves of a message together to form a sentence present in neither half. Needs a card you are willing to crease, so use it once and print a spare.
G
Sort, group and order
The endgame, and the one verb the whole deck performs together. Split by stamp, order by date, draw the legs on the four maps.
Worth building first. It proves the format to the players. Once a group has physically pushed two postcards together and watched a sentence appear, they will try it with every card in the deck for the rest of the game — which answers “where do I look?” for free on every later grouping puzzle, and turns the cards from pictures into components.

The props, re-cast

If the cards are the game, every prop’s job is now to act on a card. The comb reads a message; the magnifier reads a caption; the balance orders three cards; the board is set up by a card; the red filter and the UV pen reveal a layer on a card. The rune stick and the cryptex are the only things that never touch the deck — which is fine, since they are the one pair that should feel as though they came from somewhere else entirely.

Puzzles and clues

PZ-01 Open
Hnefatafl: three moves with printed digits, or four moves with directions?
The two are currently specified in different places and are incompatible. An exhaustive search over the layout in this page confirms exactly three moves has one solution (D4 → F4 → F7 → G7) and exactly four moves has six, so the four-move version does not work on this board. The throne compartment works either way.
PZ-02 Open

HRAFN fits a five-ring cryptex and is also currently assigned to a five-letter lock. Only one of the two can have it.
PZ-03 Open
If HRAFN goes to the cryptex, what word does the five-letter lock take?
KAMBR (Old Norse for comb) is proposed: it self-verifies, it names the comb prop so the lock opening is redirect clue 2, and it ties to Kambsnes on Aud’s trail.
PZ-04 Unwritten

Clue 1 now falls out of the continuation set (WEIGH WHAT SHE TRADED). Clue 3 is written (EVERY CARD IS A PLACE). Clue 2 depends on PZ-03.
PZ-05 Unwritten

PR-06 Deferred

HI

History

HI-01 Decided
Harald’s stamp: the labrys is the wrong axe.
The Varangian Guard were the emperor’s axe-bearing guard and carried the Dane axe — a single broad blade on a long two-handed haft. A double-headed labrys is Minoan. Artwork to be redrawn.
HI-02 Decided
Aud’s stamp: the high-seat pillars are not her story.
Casting the öndvegissúlur overboard is Ingólfr Arnarson’s, not Aud’s. Her documented motif is the comb she lost at Kambsnes, which Landnámabók says the headland is named for — and which ties the stamp to the comb prop and to KAMBR. Artwork to be redrawn.
HI-03 To fix
The branch-rune group-numbering caveat needs to be on the prop.
Real cipher-rune inscriptions commonly number the three ættir in reverse. Because it is her own key card she can use whichever she likes, but the caveat currently lives only in the designer’s notes where no player sees it. Put it on the museum label, in her voice, as a preference.
HI-04 Small fix
The Bergen rune sticks are medieval, not Viking Age.
The Bryggen rúnakefli are mostly 12th–14th century. Fine as her research; she should not call them Viking. Worth reading for PZ-05 — they are overwhelmingly business notes, ownership tags and jokes.
HI-05 Standing
Every historical claim must be source-checked before it reaches a prop.
The list is at the bottom of the Design spec tab. A review against general knowledge is not a source check.

Available

The denomination is in local currency, so it groups the cards by country as well as by trail. Gives a clean four (France), a pair (England) and six singletons — which are exactly Harald’s six, since his is the only trail that never repeats a country.
Dropped
Anomaly stamps
A card wearing the wrong trail’s motif. Its job was to select which cards matter; with every card active there is nothing to select. Two ideas if it is ever revived: the anomaly cards are the ones whose dates are wrong, or they are her own bookmarks for the three stops where she found something.
Dropped
Tilted stamps
A four-state channel that is fiddly to print consistently and hard to read reliably. If it ever comes back, the better job is bearings: lay each card on its map location and the stamp angle points somewhere, with four lines crossing at one point.

Make the bag feel like it belongs to her

Core reusable prop kit

  • Museum tickets that can carry dates, locations, exhibit references or short clue fragments.
  • A 3D-printed cryptex with rune rings; its answer and place in the sequence are still to be defined (PZ-02).
  • A 7 × 7 hnefatafl-inspired board with durable pieces, a hidden compartment under the throne square opened by the king piece, and its task set by a postcard rather than a separate rule card.
  • Four printed journey maps, one per trail, protected by lamination or a clear sleeve; use removable markers, cord or an overlay (PZ-06).
  • Hand-written notes made as replaceable inserts or reusable facsimiles, with a clean master kept for reprinting.
  • A set of 18 dated postcards, one per route stop — the spine of the game, released a few at a time. Only the first card starts outside the bag.
  • A carved rune stick for the branch-rune cipher, with its key table on a separate museum label.
  • A comb with broken teeth, used as a grille over postcard messages.
  • Replica coins and a small balance with weights.
  • Reader tools: a magnifier, a red acetate filter, and a UV pen with a blacklight.
  • Replica object labels, family record cards, saga strips, a travel wallet and a fictional boarding pass.

Design for repeat play: no writing on irreplaceable props, all loose pieces inventoried, and every puzzle given a clear reset state.

Standard

What makes a clue good here

The Hiking backpack is the calibration. Its codes sit at the intersection of two or three props, its items arrive before their purpose is known, and the part it hides is the ordering rule rather than the data. Norse should be a notch harder than that.

The three questions

Run every puzzle through these:

  • Where do I look?
  • What do I take from it?
  • In what order?

Answering all three makes the puzzle obvious. Answering none makes it unfair. Answer two, hide one. The hidden one should almost always be the third.

Hide the rule, not the data

Numbers and letters stay in plain sight. What players have to earn is which ones to use and in what sequence. This is what the Hiking sticker puzzle does: the digits are visible, the historical-era ordering is the puzzle.

Re-read, don't re-find

The cheapest strong aha is re-reading a prop already read for a different reason. Plant ordering rules as throwaway habits in the aunt's letters, then make one of them load-bearing three puzzles later.

Two or three props per code

At least two codes in the game should need three separate props: a data source, a selection rule and an ordering rule. This is the main difficulty lever and it is cheaper than adding puzzles.

Answers self-verify

Where possible the final step should produce a word or a shape that is visibly right before the lock is touched. Hiking does this with MOON.

Soft red herrings only

Nothing is a dead end. A prop that looks irrelevant now must be load-bearing later. Never spend a player's attention without eventually repaying it.

Supply every table

No outside knowledge, ever. Rune tables, coin references and board rules all ship inside the bag, the way Hiking ships a periodic table and a calculator.

Hint ladder. Hiking runs two hints per stage. Three-prop codes need three: the first purely locational ("you already have everything — one piece is a card you have already read"), the second naming the props, the third naming the rule.

Format: purchased, never sent

Each card is addressed and stamped like a real postcard rather than a plain note, which is what makes an unmailed stamp collection plausible in the first place: travellers buy postcards and never send half of them. It also earns back an address block as usable clue space, which a note-card format would give up for nothing.

The recipient is fictional: John Ericson, 24 Longship Way, Ottawa ON K1L 1S1, Canada. "Longship Way" doesn't exist as an Ottawa street, so the address can't collide with a real occupied building regardless of the postal code. Ericson doubles as a quiet nod to Leif without being tied to his trail specifically — it reads as the family surname.

The same stamp series is reused across countries within a trail; explained, if it ever needs explaining, as the aunt buying a batch of stamps at the first post office of each leg. This is ordinary traveller behaviour and doesn't need to be stated in-fiction.

First-card layout, built: the writing side is split vertically, with Aunt Liv's existing message on the left and, on the right, the Leif longship stamp and postmark, a shrunk boxed address, and a boxed, titled Fun Fact block underneath it — matching the current spec in the Postcard system tab. The stamp sits at roughly 20 × 24 mm, with a circular place-and-date mark reading 07 JUL. The underline under the day is left over from the superseded 0734 mechanism and now has no job (PC-02), and the mark itself still needs redrawing as a real cancel rather than a clean graphic (PC-10).

Parked idea. One postcard could carry a different recipient address as a "look here" signal. Still parked, and now the only surviving piece of the dropped anomaly idea — with every card active there is nothing left for such a signal to select, so it would need a new job before it is worth using.

Superseded: the sparse-channel model

This section used to specify a quiet majority of cards with data carried on small subsets — four price cards, five extra-text cards, four tilted stamps and three anomaly stamps — together with the card-by-card worksheet that assigned them.

It has been replaced by the all-active model: every one of the 18 cards does a job besides the final ordering, the data lives in a titled Fun Fact block rather than in a sparse channel, and the anomaly and tilted-stamp channels are dropped. The reasoning and the new assignment are in the Postcard system tab.

Kept as a note rather than deleted silently, because the argument it made — that scarcity is itself a signal — is sound and may be worth reviving if the deck ever grows past 18 cards.

Other postcard mechanics worth using

  • Grille overlay. A card with windows cut in it laid over a message, exposing only certain words. One grille rotated or flipped decodes several cards. See the comb prop below.
  • Hole-punch stack. Punched holes plus a registration notch; stacked in the right order against a window, only certain letters on the bottom card stay visible. The order is the puzzle, and it rehearses the skill the final map needs.
  • Edge continuation. Horizon lines that continue between cards, with something written across the seams and legible only when the joint is closed.
  • Fold lines. A crease brings two halves of a message together to form a sentence present in neither half.
  • Counting in the illustration. Boats, birds, windows. Trivial on its own — only ship it with a non-obvious ordering rule attached.

Cipher backbone

Branch runes (kvistrúnir)

A real Norse cipher form: a vertical stem with branches on either side. The left branches count which group a rune belongs to; the right branches count its position within that group. Every character is a pair of small numbers drawn as tally strokes.

Why this one

  • It reads as authentic carving. Nobody guesses it is a cipher before finding the key.
  • It is two-axis, so much harder than substitution — but mechanical once understood, so nobody stalls forever.
  • It suits a carved stick, which is a strong physical object.
  • The key splits naturally across two props.

Honest caveat for the prop. Group-numbering conventions vary between real inscriptions. Because this is the aunt's own key card, that variation is sidestepped — but the prop should say so, the same way her digit cipher already carries a note that it is not a historical rune-to-number system.

Held in reserve

  • Bind runes — two or three runes overlapped into a single glyph, to be decomposed. Best used once, as a final step rather than a system.
  • Jötunvillur — a real Norse cipher replacing each rune with the last sound of that rune's name. Strong flavour, needs a supplied name table.
  • Cistercian numerals — one glyph per number up to 9999. Not Norse; only usable framed as a Norman abbey ledger mark, which the Rouen and Bayeux stops would support.
Prop

Hnefatafl: escape path and hidden compartment

Two payoffs from one prop — a directional code from solving the board, and a postcard released by the board itself.

The rule card, in full

No complete Viking rules survive, so I wrote my own. The king slides any distance in a straight line. Dark pieces never move, and they block him. Get him to a corner in exactly four moves.

Three lines, no captures, no prior knowledge. The word exactly is what forces a unique solution. The historical framing is also true: no complete Viking-Age tafl ruleset survives, so the aunt writing her own is honest rather than a shortcut.

Output: four directions, straight into a directional lock. Do not print digits on the squares — digits on a game board announce that it is a puzzle prop.

The throne compartment

Print the board with a cavity under the throne square and make the king piece the key that opens it. Solving the board physically releases a postcard, with no lock and no code — the best tactile beat available in the bag.

Needs work The board layout must be solved, not eyeballed. A layout with a provably unique four-move solution has to be generated and verified by search before the board is printed.

Prop

The comb grille

A printed or 3D-printed comb with broken teeth, laid across a postcard message so that only certain words show through the gaps. Rotated or flipped, one comb decodes several cards.

Combs are among the most common Viking finds and broken teeth are entirely plausible, so the object hides in plain sight. The aunt's letter says "my grandmother's comb — I never travel without it." Pure sentiment, until it isn't.

Reuse it. The comb decodes cards early; later, its broken-tooth pattern reads as a tally. Reusing one prop across two stages is harder for players than a new prop and cheaper for you to build.

Tools

The three decoding tools and what each is for

Each tool opens a class of clue. Each should have one primary job so it never feels decorative.

UV pen and blacklight

Primary job: the trail reading order. The order in which the four trails are read is currently "left to right," which is arbitrary — the strongest moment in the game resting on the weakest rule. Instead, write four small numerals beside the trails on the map in UV ink. The order becomes something she left for you, and the code 1972 survives untouched.

This is the highest-value use of the pen. Spend it here before anywhere else.

Red acetate filter

Primary job: the illustrations. A red-noise wash over each postcard image; under the filter, one object per card is circled or a latitude appears in the sky. Adds a clue layer with no extra prop.

Needs work Home printers vary a great deal on this. Test colour accuracy before committing any puzzle to it.

Magnifier

Primary job: the branch runes. Carve them small enough that the tally strokes are ambiguous to the naked eye. The magnifier turns "I think that's three branches" into certainty, which makes it a real tool rather than a gimmick.

Mechanic

Three non-lock redirect clues

Puzzles whose answer is an instruction rather than a code. They work best exactly where a lock would be clumsy. Three is the cap — more and the locks stop feeling like the spine.

Rules that keep them from stalling a group

  • Name a prop that is unique. There is one comb, one balance, one board. Never "the card."
  • Name a physical action, not a concept. Weigh, hold to the light, fit into, rotate, stack. Never "consider" or "compare."
  • The payoff must be visible within seconds, or players will not believe they did it right.

Tone & voice

Carried over from the Props & choices tab's presentation direction — restated here as writing guidance, not redecided.

Playful mystery

An adventurous aunt, dry museum humour, danger kept fictional and light. Avoid horror, solemn ritual, or dense historical exposition.

Clever, not childish

Challenge comes from connecting evidence, handling objects and spotting patterns — not trivia, tiny print or obscure rune knowledge.

Mostly adults, also teens

Assume no prior Norse knowledge. Clue wording stays direct even when the props carry the atmosphere.

Difficulty lever

A notch harder than Hiking, via convergence — at least two codes needing three separate props — not obscurity.

Components

Buttons & navigation

Solid green for the primary action or the active nav tab; an outlined neutral button everywhere else. No third button style.

Tags & pills

TagPill · defaultPill · openPill · test

Tags label the whole page or a whole card (top of the header, "Decided" lists). Pills are Design-spec-tab only and carry status: green = done/default, rust = open question, brown = needs a test.

Cards & panels

The single reusable content container: cream-white fill, a thin warm-grey border, a soft corner radius, generous padding. Called .panel outside the Design spec tab and .card inside it — same look, scoped separately so the two tabs' styles can't leak into each other.

Notes & warnings

A left-border callout in place of an icon: amber border for a note worth flagging, rust border for something that actually blocks progress. Used sparingly — a page of warnings stops reading as a warning.

Tables

Minimal ruled rows, no vertical lines, small-caps-style uppercase headers in muted grey. Used for any structured comparison (stamp series, container contents, route stops) rather than building one-off layouts.

Icons & illustration

ᚱ
No generic icon set. The page uses real Younger Futhark runes as decoration (the header's giant faint watermark, the rune-choice buttons) rather than a symbol library. It reinforces that the cipher is a real writing system, not a game icon.
●
Swatch dots (.dot/.swatch) are the only abstract icon in use, for legends: small, flat, colour-coded squares identifying a trail or a design channel at a glance — as seen in the postcard corpus worksheet and the colour swatches on this page.
◆
Hand-drawn line diagrams (the postcard-back map, the hnefatafl board, the branch-rune figures) share one muted parchment palette — #f0e6cf fill, #a99a7b/#8a7d63 stroke — instead of being illustrated individually. New diagrams should reuse those two stroke tones rather than introducing new ones.
