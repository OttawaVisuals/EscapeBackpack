Working direction

A family secret, carried by postcards

Your great-aunt Liv has been tracing the family’s Nordic origins. Her first postcard arrives with her locked field bag. Solving what she left behind releases more postcards from more places.

The postcards are the game. Everything else in the bag exists to act on them: the comb reads a message, the balance orders three cards, the board is set up by one. Players use the cards as ordinary clues along the way, and at the end the whole deck becomes one final answer.

Keep the original ending

The final route code opens the last container. Inside is the plane ticket from your idea deck, plus: “I could use someone who notices what everyone else walks past.”

The ticket is a fictional game prop. The candidate routes form 1972: Leif 1, Rollo’s family line 9, Aud 7 and Harald 2. Dates remain open. The printed maps are now settled (PZ-06): four laminated letter sheets, drawn on with wet-erase marker. The dig is in Norway, but the site itself and what it is for are still open (ST-05).

Why it is locked at all

Liv loves setting family challenges. This parcel is her invitation test. Each stage protects a piece of her research and releases the journey a few places at a time.

The recurring “aha”

Every card does a job once, so players keep it. At the end every card does a second job, and the deck they have been carrying turns out to be the map of her whole trip.

This sequence is deliberately incomplete. Two anchors are settled — the first puzzle and the last. Everything between them is open, and so is the number and naming of the containers the bag is divided into. That is on purpose: the middle should be designed out of the postcard system rather than fitted around a pocket list decided in advance. Candidates live in the Options tab; everything unresolved is tracked in Open questions.

What we know

Two anchors

First puzzle · a four-digit code

The bag arrives locked, with the first postcard and two luggage tags attached to it. Each tag has a printed “If found, please return to” line that Liv never filled in properly — instead she hand-wrote the address of a hotel she stayed at, and the street number is the only number on the tag.

Postcard 01 is from L’Anse aux Meadows and names the place. The tag addressed to a L’Anse aux Meadows hotel therefore carries the first two digits; the other tag carries the last two.

Code 1021 — the year tree-ring dating placed the Norse at L’Anse aux Meadows. Tag A: Vinland Trail Lodge, 10 Skipper’s Wharf, L’Anse aux Meadows, NL. Tag B: Hôtel Rollon, 21 avenue Rollo, Rouen, France. Note what the postcard is doing: it supplies the ordering rule, not the data. That is the standard the rest of the game should hold to.

Last puzzle · four maps and every postcard

Players finish with four printed maps and all 18 postcards. Each map carries one trail. Placing that trail’s cards and drawing its legs in the right order traces a shape, and the four shapes are the four digits of the final code.

Candidate code 1972: Leif 1, Rollo 9, Aud 7, Harald 2. The shapes still have to be tested at true print size — the family 9 is the riskiest. Whether the cards are pinned, connected with cord, or read under an overlay is undecided.

The brief

A card does three jobs

1 · It is a map point

The stamp motif gives the trail; the postmark gives the date and the place. All eighteen feed the final reveal: split by stamp, order by date, draw the legs, read the four digits.

2 · It is worth reading

Real trivia about a real place, in a titled Fun Fact block (was the printed caption — renamed and moved when the address block shrank to make room for it). Written on card 01 (the butternut wood found far from any butternut tree) — the template for the other seventeen.

3 · It carries a clue

Alone or in a group. Her message sets a task, or four cards have to be physically lined up before they say anything at all.

Every card is active. All eighteen do a job besides the final ordering. This replaces the earlier “five or six active cards, the rest quiet” decision, which is why the sparse-channel worksheet has been removed from the Design spec tab.
The craft idea

Every card carries two voices

This is how real picture postcards work, and it is what lets jobs 2 and 3 sit on one small piece of card without either getting in the other’s way.

Fun Fact, typed · the publisher’s
L’ANSE AUX MEADOWS, NEWFOUNDLAND — The only confirmed Norse settlement in North America, excavated 1961–1968 by Helge and Anne Stine Ingstad. Eight timber-and-sod structures. Édition Vinland Nº 14.
Job: facts, and the numbers that hide in them. Dry, third-person, small type in the titled Fun Fact block, on all 18 — nobody suspects a caption.
Handwritten · hers
Third time here and the wind still finds the one gap in my coat. Don’t believe the eight structures — I counted seven and an argument. Ask your father about the comb.
Job: voice, tasks, and the rules. Which cards, in what order, what to do. The quirk lives entirely here.

The split does quiet work. A typed Fun Fact can carry a number without ever looking planted, because it is a genuine fact about a genuine place. And because she argues with it in her own handwriting elsewhere on the card, she can point at exactly the number she wants read without ever writing “use this number.”

Decided. Fun Fact is always typed, on every card — never handwritten. Keeps the two voices doing one job each: typed for facts and hidden numbers, her handwriting for voice, tasks and rules. PC-12 closed.

The toolbox

What a deck of cards can do that a sheet of clues cannot

If the postcards are the game, these are the game’s verbs. Each is physical, each is resettable, and each costs nothing beyond print and a pair of scissors.

A
Line four up and read across the seams
Her sentence runs off the right edge of one card and continues on the next. Self-verifying — the sentence either reads or it does not, so no lock is needed to confirm it. Worked out below.
B
Hold two together against a lamp
Print one card’s back on lighter stock; overlaid on another and lit from behind, marks from both combine into a third thing. The same instinct as a lithophane, at no cost.
C
Lay the comb across a message
The grille already in the design. Broken teeth expose certain words; rotated or flipped, one comb reads several cards. Reuse is the point — one prop across two stages is harder for players and cheaper to build.
D
Argue with the Fun Fact
Her handwriting contradicts the typed facts. Points at a number without saying “use this number,” and it is genuinely funny.
E
Set a task in the message
The card is the rule card. “The game we used to play — I have set it up as I left it. King to a corner in three. You never could see the second move.”
F
Fold one card
A crease brings two halves of a message together to form a sentence present in neither half. Needs a card you are willing to crease, so use it once and print a spare.
G
Sort, group and order
The endgame, and the one verb the whole deck performs together. Split by stamp, order by date, draw the legs on the four maps.
Worth building first. It proves the format to the players. Once a group has physically pushed two postcards together and watched a sentence appear, they will try it with every card in the deck for the rest of the game — which answers “where do I look?” for free on every later grouping puzzle, and turns the cards from pictures into components.

The dig site: a fifth gridded sheet, and what it is for.

Direction proposed: a fifth letter sheet — Liv’s excavation site plan, gridded, living in the last container with the ticket. Real digs are laid out on lettered and numbered squares, so it is the one map in the game whose grid needs no excuse; it keeps the four trail maps visually clean for the shapes drawn over them (PZ-06); and it has metres-per-square resolution where a grid on Harald’s 2,800 km map would be about 200 km per square and locate nothing.

Content idea, not yet decided: mark the squares where she found things, and make the finds the props in the bag — the bone comb, a gaming piece, the three coins, the rune stick. PZ-06 Decided
The final map mechanic: laminated letter prints, drawn on with wet-erase marker.
Four letter sheets, printed and laminated at home. Players sort the deck by stamp, lay each trail’s cards out beside its map in date order, and draw the legs straight onto the laminate. No pins, no cord, no overlay. Wipe to reset.
Pinned cards is eliminated on geometry, not preference. Rouen and Roumare are 10 km apart on a trail spanning 624 km, so they land 0.13 in apart on a letter sheet — and still only 0.38 in apart on a 24 × 36 poster. Two 5-inch postcards cannot occupy that gap at any printable size. Separately, six cards need 105 in² against a letter sheet’s 84 in² of printable area.
Footprint is fine: four letter sheets laid 2 × 2 come to about 17 × 22 in, roughly 1.4 × 1.8 ft, leaving a normal table clear for the cards and props. Larger shop-printed and laminated maps (tabloid at about 1.8 × 2.8 ft for the set) stay available if the drawing ever feels cramped, but they end free same-day reprints.
Specs: gloss pouches, since matte takes marker unevenly, and wet-erase rather than dry-erase — dry-erase smears under a palm resting on a sheet being drawn across, and ghosts if left for days. A laminating pouch also covers both faces at once, which is what PZ-07 needs for the map whose back carries the board setup. 5 mil pouches over 3 mil for a sheet that is drawn on and wiped repeatedly.
Per-trail colour, on the grid band only. Each sheet's border band carries its trail's colour — Leif #216580, Rollo #A2562D, Aud #6D528B, and #467444 for Harald once that sheet exists — with the grid references reversed to white. Four laminated sheets are hard to tell apart on a crowded table, and the band is the only element visible from any edge that carries no map content. Built into build_trail_maps_pdf.py as a band key per trail.
Print on white or natural cardstock, not coloured. The sheet is a full-coverage two-tone illustration (sea #DCE7E4, land #EFE3C4) and printers cannot lay down white, so coloured stock would shift both fills toward the paper and compress a contrast that is already narrow. That contrast is load-bearing rather than decorative: it is what identifies the unlabelled animals on Leif's chart, so it cannot be spent on route identity. Coloured stock is better spent on the paper props — her letters, the museum ticket and its index, the luggage tags, the pamphlet — where ink coverage is light and the paper colour becomes the design instead of fighting it.
Still untested: that the drawn shapes read as 1972 in a real play-test. Harald’s 2 is the loosest of the four — accepted, because by then one lock remains and the code parses as a year, which self-checks.

Every ordering reads as a vertical stroke, so a dateless deck hides nothing on a three-stop trail. It would also cost PZ-08, which takes the digit order from the postmarks.
Decided: Leif is the teaching trail. If a sharp group traces his 1 mid-game they have discovered the mechanism themselves, which is a good beat, and they hold one digit of four — still a thousand combinations. The other three trails are protected instead.
Two cards per trail are unavailable for holding back: the trail's earliest card, which PZ-08's sort needs, and its continuation-set card (Markland, Bayeux, Esjuberg, Sicily), which is needed mid-game.
Held back, chosen by drawing every one-card-removed variant:
• Rollo — Roumare forest (stop 6). The loop-closer: without it the 9 opens into a zigzag closer to a 5.
• Aud — Hvammur (stop 2). Forced by the two exclusions, and it works: any removal leaves two points and no shape.
• Harald — Aldeigjuborg / Staraya Ladoga (stop 2). Provisional. Collapses the 2 into a triangle with a tail, but Harald has no sheet in TRAILS yet so this used an approximate projection. Re-test when his map exists.
Why this works at all: the route line and numbered stop markers are drawn only on the ANSWER sheet. On the player map the stops are ordinary town dots among about 24 labels, so the cards are what identify the points — holding one back removes a point, not just a label.
Consequence for ST-01: the final container must release three postcards as well as the reward. Those three can carry the finale's instruction between them, one fragment each, which keeps every card active.
PZ-10 Decided · one piece unwritten

PR-11 Built · test print
Map decoration: ships and vignettes, and where they may go.
The maps read as competent but restrained; the wanted feel is closer to an adventure map. Decoration is Codex’s lane; the base map stays with the build script.
Art may not go just anywhere. Two hard constraints: players draw the trail legs in wet-erase over the sheet, so anything printed under that line competes with the answer; and they locate stops by reading names, so anything over a label hides a clue. NorseBackpack/TravelMap/build_art_placement_guide.py computes the legal area and renders it — green is sea clear of the route corridor, every label box and the title furniture; red is the route plus a 26pt halo where nothing prints.
Slots are cut landscape, not largest-area. Maximising area gave tall narrow columns — one was 1.62 × 4.25 in — which is the worst possible frame for a ship under sail, the main subject. The guide now constrains aspect to roughly 1.4:1–2.0:1, which costs some area and buys usable art. Slot figures re-cut a second time after PR-13’s frame pan; the numbers above this line are superseded twice over — read the build script’s LEIF_ART comments for the current ones.
Built. Six of Codex’s first-pass assets went on the sheet: longship, whale, iceberg, polar bear, wolf, small settlement cluster. Two defects were fixed on the pixels themselves rather than by asking for a re-render — fill_art_alpha_holes.py fills every fully-enclosed transparent hole (a sail, a hull, an iceberg face) with a pale paper tone, so the reference grid can no longer print through a ship that was effectively made of glass; the whale and the settlement came back untouched, since both are genuinely open line art with no enclosed body, not filled silhouettes. The longship was also shrunk, from a first pass at 2.36 × 1.61 in down to 1.70 × 0.85 in — it was crowding the Labrador Sea.
Land pieces placed by search, not by eye. Each candidate spot was required to maximise clearance from the printed coastline, then to miss every reserved label box from a clean build — not a distance-from-named-place heuristic, which first missed a real collision between the wolf and the word “MARKLAND”. Final clearances: polar bear 5.8°, wolf 1.9°, settlement 4.1° from the nearest coastline, all three also clear of the drawn route by the usual 26pt halo.
Land-only restriction relaxed for the three small pieces (bear, wolf, settlement) — see above for where they actually landed. Their boxes are reserved before town-label placement, so a name moves rather than printing through the art, and none was placed on top of a real settlement's dot. No new settlement name or map point is invented.
Style: flat screen-print, no gradients, two or three inks from the existing palette (#B56A2A, #59635D, #283B34), and reduced opacity so the art recedes behind the map information. Nothing crosses a sea-slot boundary.
Re-run the guide after any route change — the corridor moves with the stops, and art placed against an old corridor can end up under the drawn line. Exact generating prompts are recorded in the Design guide tab.
PR-12 Decided
A lettered reference grid on every map sheet.
9 × 11 cells, A–I across and 1–11 down, labels repeated on all four sides in a 12 pt border band. Cells are 0.84 × 0.86 in — visually square, and the division is exact, so there are no runt half-cells at the edges.
The grid belongs to the sheet, not to the geography. All four maps carry the identical 9 × 11, whatever frame each one uses, so a square reference means the same place on the page every time. The cost is that a square is ~190 km on Leif’s sheet and ~55 km on Aud’s; that is accepted rather than overlooked.
Drawn over the land but under every label. Crossing land is what makes it an index rather than sea decoration — a settlement can be given a square. Drawing it beneath the labels means no printed line can ever sit on top of a clue. The old 5° degree graticule is removed; this replaces it.
No puzzle uses it. Added as reference furniture, deliberately, so that if a card ever wants to name a square it is already there.
What it cost. The band takes 12 pt per side, so every sheet re-framed slightly. Two more decoy labels now collide (Caen and Falaise on Rollo, Krosshólaborg on Aud); no stop is affected. Every slot size in PR-11 changed and was recut.
Watch — Leif’s sheet now has two grids. This one on the front, the hnefatafl setup on the back (PZ-07), whose registration instruction is literally “align on the grid lines.” Different faces, different look, but see PR-09.
Watch — ST-05’s dig-site plan is gridded too. Two gridded sheets in one bag is either a family resemblance or a false lead players will burn time cross-referencing. Undecided.
PR-13 Decided
Leif’s frame panned east one grid column.
Same scale, different window. Requested as “the map moves one column left”: shift the lon window (was −69.8..−49.5) east by exactly one PR-12 grid column (2.94°) to −63.56..−37.06. Trims the empty Nunavik/Ungava Bay/Québec mass on the west and brings Greenland’s full coast into view — a pan, not a zoom; the scale bar is unchanged in km-per-inch.
Iceland was tried and dropped. At this scale Iceland’s coast sits 15.5° past even the shifted east edge — no single-column move reaches it. A separate, tighter frame (−65.5..−23.0) was built to test showing just its western tip: it works geometrically (stops safe, no leak), but was not adopted — the user asked for Frame B’s scale specifically, not a rescale.
A real bug, caught by trying the literal ask. The stops-in-frame guard checks a stop’s raw longitude against the frame’s nominal bounds. Qikiqtarjuaq (67.6°N) sits just outside those bounds after the shift and the guard correctly refused to build — but the guard was wrong to refuse: a conic projection’s meridians converge toward the pole, so the point’s actual projected position lands 1.83 in inside the printed map, verified by projecting it before trusting either the assertion or its relaxation. Both frame guards were rewritten to check the projected page position against the printed map area instead of the raw coordinate against the frame’s lon/lat box — the real question was always where the point lands on paper, not whether its coordinate reads as inside a rectangle that never gets drawn.
The pan itself still cost one region label a collision, unrelated to the guard question: HELLULAND’s label had to move (was 68.2,−68.6, now on-land at 68.5,−70.0) because its old position collided with Qikiqtarjuaq’s own label after the reframe — found by the stop-collision guard added in the grid session, which is exactly the case it exists for.
Bonus, not designed for: Brattahlíð / Qassiarsuk — Erik the Red’s estate, where Leif historically sailed from — is already in the research corpus and now falls inside the frame, so it labels for free as a decoy.
PR-10 Verify
Map label coordinates, and the coarseness of Aud’s coastline.
The printable maps are built by NorseBackpack/TravelMap/build_trail_maps_pdf.py. Most settlement labels are drawn from stops.js, the 72-entry sourced research corpus, so their coordinates are already part of the project’s vetted dataset. The remainder are curated extras marked APPROX in the config: typed from general knowledge, good to roughly a tenth of a degree, not source-checked. Why this is not merely tidy-up: two Icelandic names in the first draft — “Reyðhol” and “Hvammsýla” — were not real places. They came from faulty recall and were caught only on visual inspection. Reykholt was the real place being garbled. Every APPROX label needs checking against a source before any sheet is printed for play. Separately: Natural Earth 50m is coarse at Aud’s 4.5° frame — Breiðafjörður’s fjords are smoothed and Snæfellsnes is blocky. Acceptable as a stylised poster map, not as a survey. Finer Natural Earth data would fix it if that ever matters.
PR-08 Buy check
Hnefatafl board: bought or built — and does the throne compartment survive?
This is the real decision, and it is not really “which board” but mechanical payoff or not. A commercial set cannot have a cavity under the throne square, so buying turns the puzzle from a container that opens into a puzzle that feeds three digits to a padlock elsewhere. Middle path: buy the set for the pieces and the look, build a shallow tray for it to sit in, compartment under the board rather than under the throne — the king no longer triggers it, but a physical opening survives. Two constraints on buying: the puzzle is built for 7 × 7 (Brandubh) while 9 × 9 and 11 × 11 are the commoner sizes, and a stock 7 × 7 supplies 12 non-king pieces against the 14 blockers this layout uses. Either mismatch means rebuilding the layout and re-running the uniqueness search. Current listings have not been checked.
PR-09 Test
Does the ink offset read through the pamphlet, while the smear still hides the original?
Two halves of one print test, and the same question as PR-03. The offset must show through the pamphlet's end panel when flipped — so that panel has to be blank on both sides, on stock thin enough to read through. The smear on the map back must look like a convincing accident at arm's length without going so dark that it swallows the residual grid lines the registration depends on. Print both at true size before the artwork is finalised.
Third item, added with PZ-07’s map choice: the board setup prints on the back of Leif’s map, and that sheet is laminated. Thin stock plus lamination can let the grid ghost through to the map front — which would foul exactly the clear sea the vignette slots depend on (PR-11). Test stock weight before committing. And now in the other direction: PR-12 puts a printed reference grid on the front of that same sheet, so the test has to read both faces — a front grid ghosting onto the back would add noise to exactly the lines the offset registers against.

Punched holes plus a registration notch; stacked in the right order against a window, only certain letters stay visible. The order is the puzzle, and it rehearses the skill the final maps need.

A crease brings two halves of a message together to form a sentence present in neither half. Needs a card you are willing to crease — print a spare.

Boats, birds, windows. Trivial on its own — only ship it with a non-obvious ordering rule attached.

Her handwriting contradicts the printed facts, pointing at a number without naming it. Free wherever a card already has both voices.

The denomination is in local currency, so it groups the cards by country as well as by trail. Gives a clean four (France), a pair (England) and six singletons — which are exactly Harald’s six, since his is the only trail that never repeats a country.

Presentation direction

Playful mystery

Use an adventurous aunt, dry museum humour and satisfying discoveries. Keep danger fictional and light; avoid horror, solemn ritual or dense historical exposition.

The mystery should feel clever and curious, not childish. Let the props carry the atmosphere while clue wording stays direct.

Audience: mostly adults, also teens

Assume no prior Norse knowledge. Challenge should come from connecting evidence, handling objects and spotting patterns—not trivia, tiny print or obscure rune knowledge.

Difficulty target: a notch harder than the finished Hiking backpack. The lever is convergence — at least two codes should need three separate props — not obscurity.

Standard

What makes a clue good here

The Hiking backpack is the calibration. Its codes sit at the intersection of two or three props, its items arrive before their purpose is known, and the part it hides is the ordering rule rather than the data. Norse should be a notch harder than that.

The three questions

Run every puzzle through these:

  • Where do I look?
  • What do I take from it?
  • In what order?

Answering all three makes the puzzle obvious. Answering none makes it unfair. Answer two, hide one. The hidden one should almost always be the third.

Hide the rule, not the data

Numbers and letters stay in plain sight. What players have to earn is which ones to use and in what sequence. This is what the Hiking sticker puzzle does: the digits are visible, the historical-era ordering is the puzzle.

Re-read, don't re-find

The cheapest strong aha is re-reading a prop already read for a different reason. Plant ordering rules as throwaway habits in the aunt's letters, then make one of them load-bearing three puzzles later.

Two or three props per code

At least two codes in the game should need three separate props: a data source, a selection rule and an ordering rule. This is the main difficulty lever and it is cheaper than adding puzzles.

Answers self-verify

Where possible the final step should produce a word or a shape that is visibly right before the lock is touched. Hiking does this with MOON.

Soft red herrings only

Nothing is a dead end. A prop that looks irrelevant now must be load-bearing later. Never spend a player's attention without eventually repaying it.

Supply every table

No outside knowledge, ever. Rune tables, coin references and board rules all ship inside the bag, the way Hiking ships a periodic table and a calculator.

Hint ladder. Hiking runs two hints per stage. Three-prop codes need three: the first purely locational ("you already have everything — one piece is a card you have already read"), the second naming the props, the third naming the rule.

Format: purchased, never sent

Each card is addressed and stamped like a real postcard rather than a plain note, which is what makes an unmailed stamp collection plausible in the first place: travellers buy postcards and never send half of them. It also earns back an address block as usable clue space, which a note-card format would give up for nothing.

The recipient is fictional: John Ericson, 24 Longship Way, Ottawa ON K1L 1S1, Canada. "Longship Way" doesn't exist as an Ottawa street, so the address can't collide with a real occupied building regardless of the postal code. Ericson doubles as a quiet nod to Leif without being tied to his trail specifically — it reads as the family surname.

The same stamp series is reused across countries within a trail; explained, if it ever needs explaining, as the aunt buying a batch of stamps at the first post office of each leg. This is ordinary traveller behaviour and doesn't need to be stated in-fiction.

First-card layout, built: the writing side is split vertically, with Aunt Liv's existing message on the left and, on the right, the Leif longship stamp and postmark, a shrunk boxed address, and a boxed, titled Fun Fact block underneath it — matching the current spec in the Postcard system tab. Other postcard mechanics worth using

  • Grille overlay. A card with windows cut in it laid over a message, exposing only certain words. One grille rotated or flipped decodes several cards. See the comb prop below.
  • Hole-punch stack. Punched holes plus a registration notch; stacked in the right order against a window, only certain letters on the bottom card stay visible. The order is the puzzle, and it rehearses the skill the final map needs.
  • Edge continuation. Horizon lines that continue between cards, with something written across the seams and legible only when the joint is closed.
  • Fold lines. A crease brings two halves of a message together to form a sentence present in neither half.
  • Counting in the illustration. Boats, birds, windows. Trivial on its own — only ship it with a non-obvious ordering rule attached.

Cipher backbone

Branch runes (kvistrúnir)

A real Norse cipher form: a vertical stem with branches on either side. The left branches count which group a rune belongs to; the right branches count its position within that group. Every character is a pair of small numbers drawn as tally strokes.

Why this one

  • It reads as authentic carving. Nobody guesses it is a cipher before finding the key.
  • It is two-axis, so much harder than substitution — but mechanical once understood, so nobody stalls forever.
  • It suits a carved stick, which is a strong physical object.
  • The key splits naturally across two props.

Hnefatafl: escape path and hidden compartment

Three connected pieces — a setup split across two sheets of paper, a three-move escape, and a code read from the king's own travel.

The rules, and where they come from

The rules are printed matter, not her handwriting: a gift-shop pamphlet from the Hnefatafl Museum in Oslo, folded into the bag. That keeps the voice rule intact — printed pages carry real history, her hand carries the task. The pamphlet explains how the game is played; Liv's postcard says what to do with it.

The king slides any distance in a straight line. Dark pieces never move, and they block him. No jumping, no captures. The king escapes to a corner.

The pamphlet can say honestly that no complete Viking-Age tafl ruleset survives and that these rules are one reconstruction — which is also the cover for Liv having simplified them. Its text is subject to HI-05 before it is printed. The museum is invented deliberately; the real Oslo institution is the Viking Ship Museum, and the prop should not borrow it.

Her task, on a postcard: get him to a corner in exactly three moves, and count how far he travels each time. The word exactly is what forces the solution to be unique.

The setup: one board across two papers

Liv wrote the piece positions on the back of a trail map. The ink was still wet when the pamphlet was pressed down on it, and the right-hand strip lifted off — what is left on the map is an illegible smear, and the clean copy is on the pamphlet's blank end panel, mirrored.

  • The gap announces itself. The grid lines and column letters run into the stain, so the board is visibly seven wide and visibly missing two columns. Nobody has to deduce that a second sheet exists; the stain is the shape of the object that took the ink.
  • Reading it: flip the pamphlet and read the offset through the paper — mirrored twice, so it reads correctly — then butt it against the map. The offset must therefore land on a panel that is blank on both sides, on stock thin enough to show through (PR-09).
  • Registration is self-verifying. The grid lines cross the join, so only one alignment makes them continue. A one-square misalignment is visible rather than silent.
  • Guessing does not produce a wrong answer. Verified by search: the full board has exactly one three-move escape, but if a player assumes the hidden strip is empty they find 18 candidate routes. The puzzle refuses to resolve instead of resolving wrongly — the failure mode you want in a bag nobody can debug mid-play.

Both the smear and the offset are printed, not real ink, so the prop is stable and reprintable. This also gives the four trail maps an early job: players handle one mid-game and get used to it, without learning what it is finally for.

The escape, and the code

On the layout in the Prototype tab the unique solution is D4 → F4 → F7 → G7. The legs measure 2, 3 and 1 squares, so the code is 231.

Nothing is printed on the board. The earlier plan — digits on the landing squares, or four moves into a directional lock — is superseded (PZ-01). Reading the code off the king's own travel works on a bought board as well as a built one, and it stops the board from announcing itself as a puzzle prop.

The throne compartment, and what a bought board costs

Build the board with a cavity under the throne square and make the king piece the key that opens it. Solving the board physically releases a postcard, with no lock and no code — the best tactile beat available in the bag.

This is the one thing a commercial set cannot do, and it is the real content of PR-08. Buying a board turns this puzzle from a container that opens into a puzzle that feeds three digits to a padlock somewhere else. A middle path exists: buy the set for the pieces and the look, and build a shallow tray for it to sit in, with the compartment under the board rather than under the throne square — less elegant, since the king no longer triggers it, but it keeps a physical opening.

A bought set also constrains the layout. The puzzle is built for 7 × 7 (Brandubh); 9 × 9 and 11 × 11 are the commoner sizes, and either would mean rebuilding the layout and re-running the uniqueness search. A stock 7 × 7 also supplies 12 non-king pieces against the 14 blockers this layout uses, so the layout would need rebuilding to fit even at the right size.

Needs work The board layout must be solved, not eyeballed, and it is now downstream of two decisions. A layout with a provably unique three-move solution has to be verified by search for whichever board is bought or built (PR-08), and the map/pamphlet split has to be checked against it — the hidden strip must leave the visible board unresolvable rather than merely incomplete.

Tools

The three decoding tools and what each is for

Each tool opens a class of clue. Each should have one primary job so it never feels decorative.

UV pen and blacklight

Primary job: the trail reading order. The order in which the four trails are read is currently "left to right," which is arbitrary — the strongest moment in the game resting on the weakest rule. Instead, write four small numerals beside the trails on the map in UV ink. The order becomes something she left for you, and the code 1972 survives untouched.

This is the highest-value use of the pen. Spend it here before anywhere else.

Red acetate filter

Primary job: the illustrations. A red-noise wash over each postcard image; under the filter, one object per card is circled or a latitude appears in the sky. Adds a clue layer with no extra prop.

Needs work Home printers vary a great deal on this. Test colour accuracy before committing any puzzle to it.

Magnifier

Primary job: the branch runes. Carve them small enough that the tally strokes are ambiguous to the naked eye. The magnifier turns "I think that's three branches" into certainty, which makes it a real tool rather than a gimmick.

Mechanic

Three non-lock redirect clues

Puzzles whose answer is an instruction rather than a code. They work best exactly where a lock would be clumsy. Three is the cap — more and the locks stop feeling like the spine.

Rules that keep them from stalling a group

  • Name a prop that is unique. There is one comb, one balance, one board. Never "the card."
  • Name a physical action, not a concept. Weigh, hold to the light, fit into, rotate, stack. Never "consider" or "compare."
  • The payoff must be visible within seconds, or players will not believe they did it right.

Tone & voice

Carried over from the Props & choices tab's presentation direction — restated here as writing guidance, not redecided.

Playful mystery

An adventurous aunt, dry museum humour, danger kept fictional and light. Avoid horror, solemn ritual, or dense historical exposition.

Clever, not childish

Challenge comes from connecting evidence, handling objects and spotting patterns — not trivia, tiny print or obscure rune knowledge.

Mostly adults, also teens

Assume no prior Norse knowledge. Clue wording stays direct even when the props carry the atmosphere.

Difficulty lever

A notch harder than Hiking, via convergence — at least two codes needing three separate props — not obscurity.

Components

Buttons & navigation

Solid green for the primary action or the active nav tab; an outlined neutral button everywhere else. No third button style.

Tags & pills

TagPill · defaultPill · openPill · test

Tags label the whole page or a whole card (top of the header, "Decided" lists). Pills are Design-spec-tab only and carry status: green = done/default, rust = open question, brown = needs a test.

Cards & panels

The single reusable content container: cream-white fill, a thin warm-grey border, a soft corner radius, generous padding. Called .panel outside the Design spec tab and .card inside it — same look, scoped separately so the two tabs' styles can't leak into each other.

Notes & warnings

A left-border callout in place of an icon: amber border for a note worth flagging, rust border for something that actually blocks progress. Used sparingly — a page of warnings stops reading as a warning.

Tables

Minimal ruled rows, no vertical lines, small-caps-style uppercase headers in muted grey. Used for any structured comparison (stamp series, container contents, route stops) rather than building one-off layouts.

Icons & illustration

ᚱ
No generic icon set. The page uses real Younger Futhark runes as decoration (the header's giant faint watermark, the rune-choice buttons) rather than a symbol library. It reinforces that the cipher is a real writing system, not a game icon.
