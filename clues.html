The Clue Library

Every way we know of to hide a clue, set a task or gate a lock — collected in one place so a new backpack can be built out of proven parts instead of reinvented from scratch. Cross-project: Hiking, Norse, Aurora, Lego and whatever comes next.

Techniques are described generically, with a worked example written for one of our own games. Difficulty dots are a rough guide for adults and teens with no outside knowledge.

How to use this library

This is a parts bin, not a plan. Pick techniques that fit the story and the props you already have, then check them against the design rules below before committing to one.

The one rule that matters most. A clue is only fair if everything needed to solve it is inside the game. No outside knowledge, no guessing at the designer's intent. If a player needs to know what Younger Futhark is, the key ships in the bag.

Reading the cards

  • Difficulty dots — one dot is spotted in seconds, five dots needs a hint ladder and a patient group.
  • Needs — what it costs to build. "Print only" is free; anything else is a shopping trip or a print run.
  • Worked example — written for one of our games so the technique is concrete rather than abstract.
  • Source — where to read more. Mostly reference articles on the underlying technique, plus escape-room design writing in the Sources tab.

How to combine them

  • One technique per puzzle. Two stacked ciphers is not twice as good, it is a wall.
  • Vary the verb. If the last three puzzles were all "decode this text," the next one should be physical, spatial or social.
  • Reuse props across puzzles. A prop that does one job reads as a puzzle prop. A prop that does three jobs reads as an object.
  • Prefer self-verifying answers. If the sentence reads, it is right — no lock needed to confirm it.

Design rules worth keeping

Clue everything

Every step should be inferable from something present. The test: can you write the hint ladder without inventing a fact that appears nowhere in the game?

One aha per lock

A lock that needs three separate insights feels like three unfinished puzzles. Split it, or cut two of the insights.

Signpost the puzzle, hide the answer

Players should know something is here. What they should not know is what to do with it. Hiding the existence of a puzzle produces searching, not solving.

Fail soft

A wrong reading should fail to resolve rather than resolve wrongly. Ambiguity that produces a confident wrong answer is the worst outcome in the game.

Start easy

The first puzzle teaches the game's habits. Make it light and make it teach one thing — usually "look closer at what you already hold."

Red herrings are a tax

Deliberate dead ends mostly produce frustration, not challenge. If something is decorative, it is fine for it to be decorative. Escape-room designers argue about this; most working advice says avoid.

Sources & further reading

Each technique card links to a reference for the technique itself. These are the general design sources behind the rules and structure advice in this library.

Escape-room design

  • Peeking Behind the Locked Door: A Survey of Escape Room Facilities — Scott Nicholson, 2015. The standard survey of the form: puzzle types, room structures (linear, open, multi-linear), themes and how facilities actually build them.
  • Ask Why: Creating a Better Player Experience Through Environmental Storytelling — Scott Nicholson. On making puzzles belong to the story instead of sitting on top of it.
  • The State of Escape: Escape Room Design and Facilities — Scott Nicholson. Follow-up with more on design patterns.
  • 13 Rules for Escape Room Puzzle Design — The Codex. Practical working rules, including "clue everything."
  • Escape Room Games (Wiemker, Elumir & Clare) — a puzzle taxonomy paper: cognitive vs physical puzzles, and how they chain.
  • Top 15 Puzzle Ideas for Escape Rooms — Escape Room Tips. Commercial-room idea list, useful for spotting what is now a cliché.
  • 50 Escape Room Puzzle Ideas with Electronics — Escape Room Supplier. Mostly beyond a backpack's budget, but the magnet/reed-switch and UV sections are directly usable.

Puzzle hunts

Puzzle-hunt culture is where the deepest thinking about extraction, metapuzzles and fairness lives. Their puzzles are usually far harder than ours, but the construction principles transfer.

  • Thinking About A Puzzle — MIT Mystery Hunt / Puzzle Club. On reading the medium itself as data.
  • How to Hunt Puzzles (2023) — MIT. A teaching set covering the standard extraction tricks.
  • Puzzle Hunt Guide — Singapore Puzzle Hunt. Contains a writing guide as well as a solving guide.
  • Puzzlehunting 201 — Alex Irpan. Good on the habits experienced solvers bring, which is what our hardest puzzles are up against.
  • MIT Mystery Hunt Puzzle Index — searchable index of past puzzles by mechanic. A browsing tool when you want prior art for a specific trick.

How to use the reference links

Most technique cards link to a general reference article rather than an escape-room page, because the underlying technique is older than the genre and better documented. Treat the link as "how it works," not "how to use it in a game" — the worked example on the card is our translation.
